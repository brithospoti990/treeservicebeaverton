Where we work around Beaverton, Oregon

All of Beaverton, plus the neighboring parts of Washington County on both sides of the Sunset Highway, from Hillsboro to Tigard. Every area has its own page, because the trees, the lots and above all the rules change from one side of a street to the other.

Beaverton neighborhoods

Inside the city limits, where Beaverton's street tree permits and protected-tree rules apply.

Five Oaks and Triple Creek

The northwest corner of the city and the closest neighborhood to our office. Subdivisions from the 1970s to the 1990s, mature street trees, and lots that back onto the Willow Creek greenway and the Waterhouse power line corridor.

Central Beaverton and Old Town

Downtown and the Old Town grid south of it: the city's oldest houses, its biggest yard trees, and its smallest lots. Tight access, overhead wires and legacy maples and firs define the work.

Sexton Mountain

South Beaverton's hill. Subdivisions from the 1980s and 1990s on steep, wind-exposed slopes, with tall firs, view lots and homeowners associations that have a say in what gets cut.

Cooper Mountain and Neighbors Southwest

The city's southwest edge, from older acreage on the ridge to the new streets of South Cooper Mountain. Oregon white oaks, wind, thin volcanic soil, and a boundary between city and county that runs through the middle of it.

Murrayhill and South Beaverton

A master-planned community from the late 1980s and the newer streets of Progress Ridge. The street trees all matured at the same time, and the homeowners association has a say over most of them.

Highland and Vose

Beaverton's mid-century neighborhoods south of downtown. Single-story ranch homes on generous lots, with Douglas-firs that were saplings when the houses were built and now tower over them.

Greenway

Southeast Beaverton along Fanno Creek and Greenway Park. High water tables, fast-growing cottonwoods and ash, and creek buffers that protect trees on private lots.

West Slope, Raleigh West and Denney Whitford

Beaverton's east side, from the wooded hillside of West Slope down to Fanno Creek at Denney Road. Some of the tallest firs in the area, steep driveways, and a patchwork of city and county land.

Nearby communities

Mostly unincorporated Washington County, plus the cities of Hillsboro and Tigard, which run their own tree codes.

Tanasbourne and Rock Creek

Our own neighborhood. Tanasbourne is the east end of Hillsboro, Rock Creek is unincorporated county north of the Sunset Highway, and the tree rules change when you cross from one to the other.

Aloha

A large unincorporated community west of Beaverton along the Tualatin Valley Highway. Older homes on deep lots, tall Douglas-firs, overhead utilities and no city tree code.

Bethany

Unincorporated community north of the Sunset Highway, from the 1990s streets around Bethany Village to the new ones in North Bethany. It is also where the state confirmed emerald ash borer in 2026.

Cedar Mill

Unincorporated community around Cornell Road and Murray Boulevard, north of the Sunset Highway. Wooded lots, creek ravines and native firs and cedars that predate most of the houses.

Oak Hills

A 1960s planned community north of the Sunset Highway, listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Greenways, shared commons and sixty-year-old trees, managed under one of the most active homeowners associations in the county.

Cedar Hills

One of Oregon's largest postwar planned neighborhoods, just north of Beaverton. Small lots, seventy-year-old trees, overhead utility lines and a homes association with covenants.

Raleigh Hills

Unincorporated community east of Beaverton where Beaverton-Hillsdale Highway, Scholls Ferry Road and Oleson Road meet. Generous lots, very large firs, and the Portland city line a few blocks away.

Garden Home and Whitford

Unincorporated community southeast of Beaverton around Oleson Road and Garden Home Road. Older homes under tall firs, two creek corridors and the Portland and Tigard city lines nearby.

Hillsboro

The county seat, west of our office. Old street trees downtown, oak remnants around Orenco, newer subdivisions everywhere else, and a city code that requires a free permit for street tree work.

Tigard

The city south of Beaverton, from Fanno Creek and downtown to the slopes of Bull Mountain. Tigard's urban forestry code names six kinds of tree that need a removal permit, and most home lots have at least one to think about.

Why the address matters

A Beaverton mailing address doesn't mean you are inside the city. Large parts of ZIP codes 97003, 97006, 97007, 97078, 97225 and 97229 are unincorporated, and there the county's rules apply, not the city's. Hillsboro and Tigard each have their own street tree permits. Your property tax statement lists the city you are in, if any.

That decides whether the tree in front of your house needs a permit to remove, how high it has to clear the street, and who to call when a neighbor's tree is the problem. Each area page spells it out, and the permit checker on the home page gives a first answer in two questions.

Don't see your neighborhood? If you are between Hillsboro and Southwest Portland and north of Tigard, we almost certainly work there. Call and ask.
