How to tell if a tree is dangerous

Most big trees in Beaverton are safe, and most of the ones that fail gave notice first. You don't need training to read that notice. You need to know where to look, and which signs mean call today as opposed to keep an eye on it.

By Harry Fauver, founder of Tree Service Beaverton. Updated September 2026.

The short version

  • Risk is two things: how likely the tree is to fail, and what it would hit.
  • Call today for a new lean, soil lifting at the base, a fresh crack in the trunk or a fork, or a broken limb hanging over a place people use.
  • Have it looked at this season for a dead or thinning top, mushrooms or conks at the base, and large dead branches.
  • A lean the tree has always had, moss, lichen and sheer size are not defects.
  • Look twice a year: in leaf, and after the first big storm.

Risk is likelihood times target

A rotten tree in the middle of a field is not a hazard, because there is nothing for it to hit. A sound tree over a bedroom is not one either. A hazard is a tree with a real defect that is also within reach of something that matters: a house, a driveway, a play area, a road, a neighbor's roof. Arborists call that something the target.

So when you look at your trees, ask two questions about each. What is wrong with it, if anything? And if it, or its biggest limb, came down, what would be underneath? The trees worth worrying about are the ones where both answers are uncomfortable.

Six warning signs on a Douglas-fir

Pick a tag. The same signs apply, with small differences, to cedars, pines and spruces.

1A dead top

A fir or cedar that is dying from the top down has a problem in its roots or its water supply. In western redcedar it is usually drought stress after hot summers. In fir it can be root rot or bark beetles. A dead top is also dead weight that will eventually fall, so it needs dealing with even if the rest of the tree is kept.

2A thinning crown and a heavy crop of small cones

When you can see a lot of sky through a fir's crown, and it is covered in undersized cones, the tree is under stress. A distress cone crop is a tree putting its last reserves into seed. On Douglas-fir this is a classic sign of root disease and deserves an inspection.

3A forked top

When a conifer loses its leader, two or more side branches turn upward to replace it. The junction between them is weak, and as the new tops grow heavy they split apart, often forty or more feet above the ground. On a younger tree one leader can be removed. On an older one the tops are reduced.

4A broken limb hanging in the crown

Tree workers call these widowmakers for a reason. A limb that has snapped but lodged in the branches below it will come down eventually, often on a calm day. If it hangs over a driveway, a path or a patio, keep people out from under it and have it removed.

5Pitch streaming down the trunk, or a crack

Conifers bleed pitch to flood out insects and seal wounds. A little is normal. Heavy flows from many points, or from the base, suggest bark beetles or root disease. A vertical crack that you can see into, especially one that was not there last year, means the trunk itself is failing and is urgent.

6Mushrooms at the base, or soil lifting on one side

Mushrooms and shelf fungi on the roots or the butt of the trunk are the visible part of decay inside. Soil that is cracked, mounded or lifting on the side away from a lean means the root plate is moving right now. That is the most urgent sign on this page. Keep everyone away and call.

Broadleaf trees have their own signs

Maples, oaks, cherries and other broadleaf trees fail differently from conifers. They are more likely to drop a large limb or split at a fork than to go over whole. Look for:

  • Twin trunks with a seam of bark between them, especially with a crack below the fork.
  • Cavities and old pruning wounds that are hollow behind. A hollow is not automatically fatal, because trees carry load in their outer shell, but it needs measuring.
  • Large dead limbs over places people use.
  • Long, heavy, horizontal limbs reaching over a roof or a drive.
  • Roots cut by a trench, a new driveway or an addition within the last few years.

Things that look alarming and usually aren't

  • A lean the tree has always had. Trees that grew leaning, usually toward light, built extra wood to hold themselves. A lean matters when it is new or increasing.
  • Moss and lichen. They grow on the bark of healthy trees in our climate and take nothing from them.
  • Cedars and firs turning brown inside in fall. Conifers shed their oldest needles every autumn. Browning on the inside of the branches, evenly through the tree, is normal. Browning at the tips or the top is not.
  • Neat rows of small holes. That is a sapsucker, a woodpecker that drills for sap. It rarely harms an established tree. Ragged patches of stripped bark on an ash are another matter.
  • Swaying in the wind. Healthy trees move, and movement is how they build strength.
  • Surface roots. Inconvenient for the mower, and entirely normal for maples and many other species.

Ivy deserves a separate mention. It doesn't feed on the tree, but a heavy load of it adds weight and sail, and it hides the base and the trunk, which are exactly the parts you need to see. Cut it at the ground and let it die off.

What an inspection involves

We start with the target: what could the tree reach? Then we work from the ground up. At the base we look at the root flare, the soil, and any fungi or cavities, and sound the trunk with a mallet, since a hollow trunk rings differently from a solid one. We look at the trunk for cracks, seams and old wounds, at the forks, and at the crown for dead wood, dieback and balance. If something needs a closer look, a climber goes up.

You get a plain answer in one of four forms: it is fine, prune this, watch this and look again in a year, or it should come down, and why. Where a tree is protected or belongs to an association, we put it in writing with photographs, since that is what the city or a board will ask for.

Questions people ask

How can I tell if a tree is going to fall?

Look for a new lean, soil cracking or lifting at the base, a fresh crack in the trunk or at a fork, mushrooms or shelf fungi on the roots or lower trunk, and a dead or thinning top. Any of these on a tree that could reach a house or a driveway deserves an inspection.

Is a leaning tree dangerous?

Not if it has always leaned. Trees that grow at an angle brace themselves with extra wood. A lean that is new or getting worse, especially with cracked or lifting soil on the far side, means the roots are failing and is urgent.

Are mushrooms at the base of a tree a bad sign?

Usually, yes. Mushrooms or shelf fungi growing from the roots or lower trunk are the fruiting bodies of decay inside the wood. The tree may still have enough sound wood to be safe, but it needs to be checked.

Is it normal for my cedar or fir to turn brown inside in fall?

Yes. Conifers shed their oldest, innermost needles each autumn, and in some years it is dramatic. Even browning on the inside of the branches is normal. Browning at the branch tips or from the top down is not.

How often should large trees be inspected?

Look them over yourself twice a year, in leaf and after the first big storm. A professional inspection every three to five years is reasonable for large trees near a house, and sooner after construction, trenching or storm damage nearby.
