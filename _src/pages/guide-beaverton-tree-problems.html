Common tree problems in Beaverton, by species

A handful of species make up most of the trees in Beaverton yards, and each has a characteristic way of getting into trouble. If you know which tree you have, you know most of what to watch for.

By Harry Fauver, founder of Tree Service Beaverton. Updated September 2026.

The short version

  • Douglas-fir: root rot. Watch the top, the cones and the base.
  • Western redcedar and birch: drought stress, dying from the top down.
  • Ash: emerald ash borer, confirmed in Beaverton in 2026.
  • Bigleaf maple: hidden decay and heavy limbs.
  • Pear, sweetgum and arborvitae: ice breakage.
  • One cause sits behind many of these: hotter, drier summers.

Pick your tree

Eight species, and what to watch for on each.

Douglas-fir: root rot

Laminated root rot and honey fungus decay the roots of firs while the crown stays green, which is why apparently healthy firs fall in winter storms. Warning signs are a thinning top, a sudden heavy crop of small cones, mushrooms or conks at the base, and nearby firs that have already gone over with almost no roots attached. Root rot spreads from tree to tree through root contact, so when one fir in a group has it, the neighbors need checking.

Western redcedar: top dieback

Cedars across western Oregon have been dying back from the top since the mid-2010s, and researchers tie it to hotter, drier summers. It hits hardest on exposed sites and in compacted or shallow soil. A cedar that is thinning can be helped with slow, deep watering in July and August and mulch over the root zone. One with a fully dead top rarely recovers, and the dead part will need to come out.

Birch: bronze birch borer

This native beetle attacks birches weakened by heat and drought, which in the Tualatin Valley means most European white birches planted in lawns. It kills from the top down: a few bare branches at the top one year, the top third the next. Once a third of the crown is gone the tree is usually finished. Keeping birches watered in summer is the only real prevention.

Ash: emerald ash borer

Confirmed in Beaverton and at Bethany Lake Park in August 2026. It kills nearly all untreated ash within a few years. Look for a crown thinning from the top, sprouts on the trunk, woodpecker damage and D-shaped exit holes. Healthy ash can be protected by a licensed applicator. See our emerald ash borer guide.

Bigleaf maple: decay and heavy limbs

Our biggest native broadleaf grows fast, reaches enormous size and decays readily at old wounds. Large limbs fail at hidden rot pockets, and twin trunks split where bark is trapped between them. Bigleaf maples have also shown crown decline in recent drought years. Most of the risk can be managed with dead wood removal and reduction of the longest limbs.

Ornamental pear and sweetgum: ice breakage

These were the street trees of the 1980s and 1990s. Both grow many upright limbs from one point, with weak, bark-filled forks, and both come apart in ice storms and wet snow. Reduction pruning before winter helps. A tree that has already split usually isn't worth keeping. Sweetgum also lifts sidewalks, and pear is increasingly invasive.

Arborvitae hedges: splaying and browning

Multi-stemmed arborvitae peel open under ice and rarely close again. They brown out from root rot in wet, poorly drained clay, and from drought in hot summers, and they never regrow from bare wood. Keep hedges to a manageable height, shear only into green growth, and tie the stems of tall ones before winter.

Oregon white oak: decline after disturbance

Our native oak is tough, long-lived and intolerant of change. Lawn irrigation under the canopy, soil piled over the roots, paving and trenching all lead to a slow decline that shows up years later. The cure is mostly on the ground: stop watering under it, keep the root zone undisturbed, and prune only dead and hazardous limbs.

Where each problem concentrates

Pick a tag. Green dots link to our page for that area.

1Bethany Lake and the Rock Creek greenway: emerald ash borer

The state confirmed the borer at Bethany Lake Park in 2026. The greenway running west from there is lined with Oregon ash, and so are the yards that back onto it. This is where ash decline will show first. See Bethany and Tanasbourne and Rock Creek.

2The Fanno Creek corridor: wet-ground failures

From Garden Home through Greenway to Tigard, the creek's floodplain grows cottonwood, alder, willow and ash on saturated soil. They root shallowly, grow fast and fall over whole in winter wind. Trees inside the creek buffer are regulated. See Greenway.

3Cooper Mountain and Sexton Mountain: wind

The hilltops take the full force of south and east winds, over thin soil. Firs left exposed by new building are the usual casualties. Cooper Mountain is also where Oregon white oak survives, and where it is most often harmed by landscaping. See Cooper Mountain and Sexton Mountain.

4Cedar Mill and the West Hills edge: cedar dieback and ravine trees

The most heavily wooded part of our area has the most western redcedar, and so the most top dieback. Its creek ravines add slope stability to the list of things to think about before removing a tree. See Cedar Mill.

5The valley floor subdivisions: street trees and ice

The flat ground from Aloha through Five Oaks to central Beaverton was built out with pear, sweetgum, red maple and ash in narrow planting strips. Cold air pools here in an ice storm, and January 2024 showed the result. See Five Oaks and Aloha.

The cause behind many of them: summer drought

Western Oregon's summers have always been dry, and its native trees are adapted to that. What has changed is how hot and how long. The heat dome of June 2021, when Portland reached 116 degrees, scorched conifers across the valley, and several of the summers since have set records of their own. Trees that were coping are now stressed, and stressed trees are the ones that bark beetles, borers and root diseases finish off.

You can't change the weather, but you can take the edge off it for the trees that matter most to you.

  • Water established trees deeply and rarely. In July, August and early September, a slow soak under the outer half of the canopy every two to three weeks does more than frequent sprinkling. Leave a hose trickling and move it a few times.
  • Mulch. Three inches of wood chips from a foot off the trunk out to the drip line keeps soil cool and moist. Arborist chips are ideal.
  • Except under native oaks. Oregon white oak is adapted to dry summers, and summer watering under its canopy invites root disease.
  • Don't prune heavily in a drought year. The tree needs its leaves.

The problem we cause ourselves: root damage

More mature trees in Beaverton are killed by construction than by any insect. A tree's roots spread two to three times as wide as its branches, mostly in the top eighteen inches of soil. A trench for a utility line, a new driveway, a patio, a change of grade of a few inches, or a summer of trucks parked under the canopy can remove or suffocate a large share of them in a day.

The tree looks fine that year. Decline shows up two to five years later, as a thin crown, dead branches or a tree on the ground after a storm, by which time nobody connects it with the work. If you are planning a project near a tree you want to keep, fence off as much of the area under its branches as you can, route trenches around it, and have it looked at before work starts, not after.

When to call someone

  • A top that is dead or noticeably thinner than last year, on any species.
  • Mushrooms, conks or oozing at the base of the trunk.
  • A lean that is new, or soil lifting on one side.
  • An ash of any size, now that the borer is here.
  • Any large tree within reach of the house that has never been inspected.
  • Before building, trenching or regrading within the spread of a tree's branches.
