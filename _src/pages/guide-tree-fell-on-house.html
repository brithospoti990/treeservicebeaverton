A tree fell on your house or car: what to do in the first 24 hours

It is loud, it usually happens at night, and for a few minutes nobody knows what to do. This is the order to do things in. If anyone is hurt or trapped, or you smell gas or see wires down, stop reading and call 911.

By Harry Fauver, founder of Tree Service Beaverton. Updated September 2026.

The short version

  • People first. Get everyone out of the rooms under the tree and keep them out.
  • Wires down or touching the tree: stay 50 feet back, call 911 and PGE at 503-464-7777.
  • Photograph everything before anything is moved.
  • Call your insurer early. You are expected to prevent further damage, and they will tell you how.
  • Don't cut anything yourself. A tree resting on a structure is under load and moves when cut.

The first ten minutes

  1. Account for everyone, including pets, and move them to the side of the house away from the tree. Call 911 for any injury.
  2. Stay out of damaged rooms. A trunk that has stopped moving can still settle, and a broken rafter can let go later.
  3. Check for wires. If any line is down, sagging or touching the tree, treat it as live. Stay at least 50 feet away, which is PGE's own rule, and call 911 and PGE at 503-464-7777. If a line is on a car with someone inside, they should stay in the car until the line is dead.
  4. Smell gas? Leave. Don't switch anything on or off. Call 911 and NW Natural from outside.
  5. Turn off power to damaged rooms at the panel, but only if you can reach it without walking under the damage or through water.

Only after those five do you call a tree service. When you call us, we ask what the tree is touching, whether wires are involved, whether everyone is out, and how we get a truck to it.

The first day, week and month

Make it safe and record it

Once everyone is safe, take photographs from a safe distance, including wide shots that show the whole tree and the house. Call your insurer's claims line. Most have a 24-hour number, and they will tell you what emergency work they authorize. Then call a tree service. Do not climb on the roof, and do not start cutting.

Get the weight off and the weather out

The emergency job is to take the tree off the structure in pieces, from the top of the pile down, without loading the roof again. It is not to clean up the whole yard. Once the tree is off, the opening needs covering against rain. Keep every receipt, because reasonable emergency measures are normally part of the claim. If the mast or meter was pulled off the wall, call an electrician today.

Adjuster, estimates and the rest of the tree

The adjuster visits or reviews your photos. Get written estimates for the remaining tree work and for the building repair as separate jobs. Ask whoever removes the tree to look at the ones still standing: a tree that fell because of root rot or saturated soil often has neighbors in the same condition.

Repairs and the things people forget

Structural repairs begin. The stump and root plate are dealt with once the debris is gone: an uprooted stump can sometimes be set back in its hole, or it is cut free and removed. If the tree was a street tree or a protected tree, tell the city what happened and show your photos. Replanting can wait for fall.

The five photographs an adjuster wants

Pick a tag. Take them before anything is moved, and again after the tree is off.

1The whole scene

Stand back, at the street if you can, and take wide shots that show the whole tree, the whole house and how they relate. Take them from two or three directions. These establish what happened better than any number of close-ups.

2The point of impact

Photograph where the trunk and the large limbs meet the roof, the gutters and the walls, from the ground with your phone's zoom. Do not go up a ladder. After the tree is lifted off, photograph the same spots again.

3The base of the tree

Show how the tree failed: a root plate tipped out of the ground, a trunk snapped partway up, or a break at an old decayed wound. It matters if there is later a question about whether the tree was sound, particularly when it came from a neighbor's lot.

4Inside the house

Ceilings, walls, floors and belongings under the damage, taken from the doorway without going under anything that is sagging. Include water coming in, since water damage spreads quickly and is part of the claim.

5Everything else it hit

Fences, sheds, vehicles, play equipment, garden walls and the neighbor's property. Vehicles are usually claimed on the auto policy, not the homeowner's, so photograph them separately and note the plate.

How insurance usually treats a fallen tree

We are not insurers and policies differ, so treat this as the general pattern and confirm the details with yours.

  • Tree hits a covered structure such as the house, the garage, a shed or a fence: removal from the structure and repair are normally covered.
  • Tree blocks the driveway or an access ramp: often covered, even with no structural damage.
  • Tree falls in the yard and hits nothing: removal is usually not covered.
  • Tree hits your car: that goes through the comprehensive part of the auto policy.
  • Hauling debris and grinding the stump are often treated differently from getting the tree off the house. We itemize the invoice that way so the adjuster doesn't have to guess.

When it was the neighbor's tree

People expect the owner of the tree to be responsible. Usually, when a healthy tree comes down in a storm, each owner's own insurer covers the damage on their own side. The neighbor becomes liable only if they knew, or should have known, that the tree was dangerous and did nothing. That is why a dated, written note to a neighbor about a dead or leaning tree, sent before the winter, carries real weight afterward. The same applies in reverse if the tree is yours.

Choosing help in a hurry

Big storms bring out-of-town crews who go door to door. Some are fine. Many are not, and the morning after a storm is a poor time to find out which. A few checks take five minutes:

  • Look up the contractor's license. Oregon requires tree services to hold a Construction Contractors Board or Landscape Contractors Board license, and both boards have an online search. No number, no job.
  • Ask for proof of liability and workers' compensation insurance, sent from the agent, not shown on a phone.
  • Get the scope in writing before work starts, even if it is two lines: what comes off the house today, and what is left for later.
  • Don't sign over your insurance claim to a contractor, and don't pay in full up front.
  • Be suspicious of anyone who suggests topping your remaining trees while they are there.
