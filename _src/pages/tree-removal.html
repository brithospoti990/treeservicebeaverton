How a tree comes down in a tight yard.

Step through a sectional removal.

1. Set up

Before a saw starts, the crew walks the drop zone, lays mats over the lawn and beds, moves what can be moved and sets a rope high in the tree. If a street tree or a protected tree is involved, the permit is already in hand.

2. Limbs, lowest first

The climber works up the trunk, taking limbs off on the way. Anything over a roof, a fence or wires is tied off and lowered by the ground crew. Limbs are only dropped free where there is nothing underneath to hit.

3. The top

With the limbs gone, the top is as light as it will ever be. It is tied off, cut, and swung down beside the trunk under control. This is the moment people worry about, and it is usually the quietest part of the day.

4. Trunk in sections

The bare trunk comes down in short pieces. Near the house they are lowered on the rope. Over open ground they are dropped onto mats so the lawn doesn't crater.

5. Stump and cleanup

The last cut leaves the stump low and level. Brush goes through the chipper, wood is hauled or cut to the length you want, and the yard is raked and blown. Grinding the stump is a separate line on the estimate, so you can take it or leave it.

When a tree has to come down, and when it doesn't

Removal is permanent, so it should be the last answer, not the first. A good share of the trees we're called to remove can stay: a heavy limb gets reduced, deadwood comes out, a split-prone fork gets cabled, and the tree carries on for another twenty years. When that is the case we say so, even though it's the smaller job.

Removal is the right call when the tree is dead or clearly dying, when decay or root loss has left too little sound wood to trust over a house or a sidewalk, when a storm has split the main structure, or when the tree has simply outgrown a spot it should never have been planted in, such as a Douglas-fir four feet from a foundation.

What goes wrong with Beaverton's common trees

  • Douglas-fir. The big risk is root rot. A fir can keep a green crown while the roots that anchor it are gone, and then the whole tree goes over in a wet, windy week. Warning signs are a thinning top, a heavy crop of small cones, and neighboring firs that have already fallen with almost no roots attached.
  • Bigleaf maple. Fast, heavy and prone to hidden decay. Large limbs fail at old wounds, and twin trunks split where bark is trapped between them.
  • Western redcedar. Hot, dry summers have been killing cedars from the top down across the Tualatin Valley. A cedar with a dead top rarely recovers.
  • Birch. Bronze birch borer kills the upper crown first. By the time the top third is bare, the tree is usually finished.
  • Ash. Emerald ash borer reached Beaverton in 2026. Dead ash becomes brittle quickly, which makes a late removal harder and more dangerous than an early one. See our emerald ash borer guide.

Six signs a tree is becoming dangerous

Pick a tag. None of these is an automatic removal, but each is a reason to have the tree looked at before the next storm, not after.

1Dieback from the top

A crown dying from the top down means the roots or the trunk's plumbing are failing. In birch suspect bronze birch borer, in ash assume emerald ash borer until shown otherwise, and in western redcedar it is often drought. Deadwood can be pruned out once. If it keeps coming, plan the removal while the wood is still sound enough to climb.

2A crack below twin trunks

Two trunks of equal size trap bark between them instead of growing wood, so the union is weak from the start. Ice and wind pry it apart. Without a crack, weight reduction and sometimes a cable will hold it. With a visible crack it is an emergency: keep people out from under it and call.

3A cavity in the trunk

A hollow is not a death sentence, because a tree carries its load in the outer shell. What matters is how much sound wood is left for the trunk's size, whether the opening is growing, and what the tree would land on. We sound the trunk and measure before recommending anything.

4Mushrooms or shelf fungi at the base

Those are the fruiting bodies of decay that is already inside the roots or the butt of the trunk. On Douglas-fir, root rot can leave a full green crown on a tree with almost nothing holding it up. This is the sign people most often ignore and most often regret.

5A new lean, or soil lifting on one side

A tree that has always leaned grew that way and braced itself. A lean that is new, soil cracking or mounding on the side away from the lean, or roots showing after a storm mean the root plate is moving. Keep everyone clear and treat it as an emergency.

6Roots cut by a trench or a new driveway

Digging inside the spread of the branches can take out a large share of the anchoring roots in an afternoon. The tree looks fine that year. Dieback or a blowdown follows one to five years later. If a trench has gone in near a big tree, have the tree assessed before the winter storms.

How we take trees down in tight yards

There are two ways to remove a tree. Felling it whole is fast and clean, and it needs a clear lane longer than the tree is tall with a margin on both sides. Few Beaverton backyards have that. So most of our removals are sectional: a climber takes the tree apart from the top down, and every piece that could hit something is lowered on a rope.

Rigging

The rope runs through a block set high in the tree and down to a friction device at the base, so one person on the ground can lower a limb that weighs as much as a motorcycle and stop it a foot above your roof. When the tree itself is too decayed to rig from, we rig from a neighboring tree, or we take smaller pieces.

Protecting the yard

Mats and plywood go down where wood lands and where the crew walks. Irrigation heads, landscape lighting and anything else you point out gets flagged. Fence panels can be taken out and put back when that is the safer route for the wood.

Trees near wires

The insulated line running from the pole to your house can be dropped for the day by Portland General Electric, and we schedule that with them. The bare high-voltage lines along the street are different. If a tree is in those, PGE's line-clearance crews make it safe before we touch it.

Permits: check before you book

Inside the city, removing a street tree always takes a permit, and the permit normally requires a replacement from the city's approved list. Most ordinary backyard trees need none. Trees in significant groves, natural resource areas and approved landscape plans are protected, and so are designated significant and historic trees. In unincorporated Aloha, Cedar Mill, Bethany and Cedar Hills the city's rules don't apply and the county's do.

We check the tree's status before the job goes on the calendar. The permit checker gives you a first answer in two questions, and the permit guide has the detail.

The wood, the chips and the stump

You decide what stays. The written scope spells it out so there is no argument at the end of the day.

  • Wood. Hauled away, or left in rounds cut to stove length for you to split. Fir and maple both burn well once they have dried for a year.
  • Chips. Hauled, or left in a pile as free mulch. Keep them a few inches back from trunks and siding. Ash wood and chips stay inside the emerald ash borer quarantine area, which covers all of Washington County.
  • Stump. Left low and level, ground below the lawn, or dug out completely where a patio, a footing or a new tree is going. Fir and cedar stumps don't resprout. Maple, cottonwood, cherry and plum do, so those are worth grinding.

Before the crew arrives

  • Move cars out of the driveway and away from the curb in front.
  • Unlock the gate and tell us about dogs.
  • Point out sprinkler lines, lighting wire, invisible dog fences and, on county lots, the septic tank and drain field.
  • Let the neighbors know. If we need their side for access or rigging, we ask them ourselves.

Neighbors and property lines

A tree whose trunk stands on the line belongs to both owners, and both have to agree before it comes out. Limbs from a neighbor's tree that hang over your side can be trimmed back to the line, but not in a way that kills or destabilizes the tree. Oregon's timber trespass law allows double or even triple damages for cutting someone else's tree. When neighbors can't agree, the Beaverton Center for Mediation and Dialogue offers free mediation for tree disputes.
