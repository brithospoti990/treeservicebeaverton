Tree service in Oak Hills, Oregon

Oak Hills was laid out in the mid-1960s as a complete community, with houses and townhomes arranged around shared greenways, a recreation center and a school. In 2013 it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places as a mid-century planned community, the first of its kind in Oregon to be recognized that way.

The trees of Oak Hills

ZIP 97006. Unincorporated Washington County, with a Beaverton mailing address.

The design put trees at the center. Native oaks and firs were kept where they stood, the commons were planted generously, and sixty years later the canopy is the neighborhood's most valuable feature and its biggest maintenance item.

The trees are all about the same age, which means they are all reaching the same stage together. The planted maples, sweetgums, birches and pines are mature to over-mature. Birches are dying of bronze birch borer, sweetgums break in ice, and pines planted close to houses have outgrown their spots.

The native trees are older still. The Oregon white oaks that gave the community its name and the Douglas-firs kept from the original woodland are the ones most worth careful work. Oaks in particular dislike summer irrigation and soil disturbance under their canopy, both of which are hard to avoid in a landscaped yard.

Because the greenways run behind most lots, a large share of the trees that shade Oak Hills homes stand on association land. That decides who pays attention to them, who approves work on them, and who is responsible if one fails.

Around Oak Hills

The commons and greenways

The shared open space behind the houses is the association's property, and so are its trees. If one overhangs your lot or looks unsafe, report it to the association in writing. You may not prune or remove it yourself.

Around Oak Hills Elementary

The school sits in the middle of the community beside the recreation center, surrounded by some of the original oaks and firs. It is the easiest place to see how large the neighborhood's native trees have become.

The townhome clusters

Attached homes share walls, courtyards and landscape strips. A tree in a courtyard can belong to one owner, to several, or to the townhome association, and it is worth finding out which before requesting an estimate.

The Sunset Highway edge

Homes along the south side back onto the highway corridor and its sound walls. Trees there screen noise and take the east wind that funnels along the highway in winter.

Which tree rules apply in Oak Hills

Oak Hills is unincorporated, so no city tree code applies. In practice the homeowners association is the rule-maker here.

In unincorporated Washington County

No city tree code applies here, even with a Beaverton or Portland mailing address. The county generally leaves trees on a developed home lot to the owner, with two big exceptions: mapped Significant Natural Resource areas, and the vegetated corridors along creeks and wetlands that Clean Water Services regulates. Cutting inside either can need review, even for a dead tree.

Trees along the road are generally the adjoining owner's responsibility, and a county right-of-way permit is needed when planting or removal affects traffic. Many subdivisions add a layer of their own, with covenants and common-area trees that need the homeowners association's approval. For neighbor disagreements over trees, the county points residents to free community mediation.

Specific to Oak Hills

The National Register listing recognizes the community's design and does not by itself regulate what you do with a tree in your yard. The association's covenants and design guidelines do. They cover common-area trees entirely and can cover visible changes to private lots. Ask the association before removing a significant tree, and get the answer in writing. We can supply the written assessment that boards usually want.

The permit checker gives a first answer in two questions, and the permit guide has the detail. We check every tree's status before a job is booked.
