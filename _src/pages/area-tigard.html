Tree service in Tigard, Oregon

Tigard lies directly south of Beaverton across Scholls Ferry Road. Fanno Creek runs through the middle of it, the Tualatin River forms its southern edge, and Bull Mountain rises on the west side. Each of those has its own kind of tree and its own kind of tree problem.

The trees of Tigard

ZIPs 97223 and 97224. City of Tigard, with unincorporated pockets on Bull Mountain and in Metzger.

Tigard also has one of the more detailed tree codes in the county. It doesn't regulate every backyard tree, but it names six categories that need a removal permit, and it is worth knowing them before you plan any work.

Along Fanno Creek and its tributaries the trees are Oregon ash, alder, cottonwood and willow on wet ground. Much of that land counts as sensitive lands under the city's code, which protects native trees there. It is also prime ground for emerald ash borer, now confirmed just to the north in Beaverton.

Bull Mountain is the opposite: dry, exposed and steep, with Douglas-fir and some Oregon white oak left among subdivisions built from the 1990s on. Wind is the main force on the upper slopes, and steep slopes are another of the city's sensitive land categories.

In between are forty- and fifty-year-old neighborhoods with mature street trees, and newer ones where the trees were required by the development's approval. Those development-required trees stay protected long after the builder has gone.

Which tree rules apply in Tigard

Tigard has its own urban forestry code, and it differs from Beaverton's in an important way: it lists exactly which trees need a permit.

Inside Tigard city limits

Tigard's urban forestry code lists six kinds of tree that need a removal permit: street trees, median trees, heritage trees, trees planted with the city's Urban Forestry Fund, trees required by a development approval, and native trees on sensitive lands. Sensitive lands means steep slopes, the 100-year floodplain, stream corridors, wetlands and significant habitat areas.

Other trees on a single-family lot can come out without a permit. Street trees usually have to be replaced, and they must clear 8 feet over sidewalks and 13 to 18 feet over the street, depending on the street type. On apartment, commercial and HOA land, assume the trees were required by the development and ask the city first.

Specific to Tigard

Simple cases, such as a dead street tree, are decided by city staff. More complex ones go to a board. The city can also require a replacement tree or a contribution to its tree fund. Bull Mountain and Metzger include unincorporated pockets where the county's rules apply instead. Tigard also designates heritage trees, which need a permit wherever they stand.
