The best time to prune trees in western Oregon

Most pruning advice was written for places with cold, dry winters. Ours are mild and wet, and that shifts a few of the rules, especially for cherries and plums. Here is the calendar we work to in the Beaverton area, and the reasoning behind it.

By Harry Fauver, founder of Tree Service Beaverton. Updated September 2026.

The short version

  • Most shade trees: December through February, while they are dormant.
  • Cherries, plums and other stone fruit: in the dry months, June through August.
  • Spring-flowering trees: right after they bloom.
  • Avoid big cuts in October and November, and while leaves are opening in spring.
  • Dead, broken and hazardous limbs: any day of the year.

The calendar

A calendar like this is a guide to what is ideal, and it matters most for large cuts and heavy pruning. Taking a few small branches off a healthy tree in the wrong month does no lasting harm. Taking a third of the crown off at the wrong time can.

December through February.

With the leaves off you can see the structure, the tree isn't spending energy on growth, and it has the whole spring to begin closing its wounds. Light corrective pruning in summer is fine. Avoid the weeks when leaves are opening, when the tree's reserves are at their lowest.

Midsummer, or the first half of winter.

Maples and birches pruned in late winter drip sap for weeks. It looks alarming and does the tree no real harm. If it bothers you, prune in June through August once the leaves have hardened, or in December and early January before the sap rises. Birches stressed by bronze birch borer should be pruned as little as possible.

Late winter, February into March.

Prune for structure and fruiting wood while the tree is dormant, late enough that the worst of the cold and wet is past. Summer pruning in July and August is a useful second visit for controlling size and removing water sprouts, because it slows the tree instead of stimulating it.

In dry weather, June through August.

This is the biggest difference from advice written elsewhere. In our wet winters, fresh cuts on stone fruit invite bacterial canker and fungal diseases that enter through wounds and can kill whole limbs. Prune after harvest, in a dry spell, so the cuts dry and seal before the rain returns. The same applies to ornamental flowering cherries and plums.

Right after it finishes blooming.

These trees set next year's flower buds during summer. Prune in winter and you cut off the display. Prune in the few weeks after flowering and you get both good structure and next year's bloom. Dogwoods are prone to anthracnose in wet springs, so keep cuts small and tools clean.

Winter, or late summer.

Conifers are best pruned when they are not pushing new growth, which means avoiding April through June. Remove whole limbs back to the trunk. Conifers don't resprout from bare wood, so a branch cut halfway stays a stub. Pines can be kept compact by pinching the new candles in late spring, which is a separate technique.

Late spring to midsummer, after the first flush.

Shear once the spring growth has firmed up, usually in June, and again lightly in July if needed. Stop by early August, because growth pushed out late doesn't harden before frost. Laurel and photinia can be cut back hard in late winter. Arborvitae can't, since it won't regrow from brown wood.

Winter, and sparingly.

Our native oak needs little pruning beyond dead wood and clearance, and it is best done while dormant. Oregon doesn't have oak wilt, the disease behind the strict oak pruning seasons in the Midwest and Texas, so the timing is about the tree's energy, not infection. What happens on the ground under an oak matters far more than any pruning.

Why timing matters more here than elsewhere

Wet winters spread disease

From October to April, rain splashes bacteria and fungal spores onto every fresh wound, and cool temperatures mean the tree closes those wounds slowly. That is the reason behind summer pruning for stone fruit, and the reason autumn is the poorest season for large cuts on almost everything. Decay fungi release most of their spores in the fall rains.

Spring leaf-out is expensive

A tree spends its stored reserves pushing out leaves, and doesn't start earning them back until those leaves are full size. Heavy pruning in April and May removes the new leaves the tree has just paid for. Bark also slips and tears easily in spring.

Dry summers bring their own limit

July through September is reliably dry, which is good for wounds and hard on trees. Don't prune heavily during a heat wave or a drought year. A stressed tree needs every leaf it has.

Birds nest from March to July

Active nests of most native birds are protected by federal law. We check before we cut and work around a nest until the young have left, which is usually a matter of a few weeks.

How much, and how

When you prune matters less than how. A well-placed cut in the wrong month is better for the tree than a bad cut in the right one.

  • Cut at the branch collar, the swollen ring where a branch joins the trunk. Not flush with the trunk, and not leaving a stub.
  • Take no more than about a quarter of the live crown in a year, and much less from an old or stressed tree.
  • Never top. It doesn't matter what month it is.
  • Don't paint the wounds. Sealants don't prevent decay and can trap moisture.
  • Start young. A few small cuts in a tree's first ten years prevent the large, risky ones later.

Three timing myths

"Prune in fall, once the leaves are down." It feels tidy, and it is the worst season for big cuts here: the wettest weather, the most fungal spores, and a tree that is shutting down and won't respond until spring. Wait until December.

"Never prune in summer." Summer is the right season for stone fruit, a good one for maples and birches, and a useful one for slowing an over-vigorous tree. The limit is heat and drought, not the calendar.

"It has to wait for the right month." Not if it is dead, broken, cracked or hanging. A hazardous limb over a roof or a walkway comes out the week you find it.
